Chapter 1 · A day in the field

Maintenance that doesn't wait for signal.

A telecom field technician keeps the network on air: towers, rooftops, exchanges, fiber cabinets. The work happens where the equipment is, and the equipment is exactly where a phone doesn't work - often because the site being repaired is the coverage.

Steel equipment shelters

The shelter at the base of a tower is a steel box full of RF gear. It is a Faraday cage: zero bars with the door shut.

The site is off-air

Corrective maintenance means the thing providing coverage here is the thing that's broken.

Basement telecom rooms

Central office power rooms sit on level B1 behind fire doors, under reinforced concrete.

Mountaintop relays

Hudson Valley: six miles of access road with no coverage, to a microwave relay that is the only link for the valley behind it.

Cable vaults and manholes

Fiber splicing happens below street level, in a vault with a steel lid.

Rooftop plant rooms

Under the antennas, behind the lift motor room, one bar of LTE that drops the moment a form submits.

Friday, 8:30 am - Salem Harbor Tower (BOS-0417), Salem MA

Maya Chen is inside the equipment shelter of a Critical-SLA macro tower. Two alarms are open: battery string A conductance low, and shelter high temperature. The app already knows all of this - the asset-health scores landed on her phone at 5 am, with a work plan: two corrective tasks, a six-step preventive checklist, and six recommended parts. Four battery blocks and the rest come to $1,897, which the app tells her is $377.40 over what's left of the site's quarterly maintenance budget, and that the depot only has three of the four blocks. She ticks off the checklist, submits it as a budget override with a reason. The phone says On device. Twenty minutes later, at the gate, it says Awaiting approval - and by the time she reaches the central office, Work order WO-261113-… closed, 1 part on order, both alarms cleared.

Talking points. Field service is an offline problem first and an AI problem second. Every alarm, asset, part and procedure the technician needs is on the phone before the visit; nothing inside the shelter waits on a network. The AI assistant and the governance come along for free once the data plane is right.
Chapter 2 · What the app needs

Eight requirements. Four for the phone, four for the AI - and the Databricks bill.

On the phone
  • Every site in the area, on device. Alarms, asset health, access notes, SLA class, budget, work history and the model's work plan - before the technician opens the gate.
  • Preventive and corrective work in one work order, with no signal. Checklist, parts, costing, the budget check and the service-impact check all compute locally.
  • Sync when the radio comes back. Work orders go up; completions, parts on order and approvals come down, to the same card the technician tapped.
  • One source of truth per area. Two technicians can't fit the same last battery block; a site has one budget and one alarm list.
For the AI - and the data platform
  • Grounded in today's export, and only in this site's profile - never a neighbouring tower's, never another area.
  • Repeat questions are cheap. The second technician to ask "how do I test a battery string?" costs nothing; the same site question twice costs nothing.
  • Actions are governed. Work orders, budget overrides and outage windows run as audited tool calls; overrides wait for Field Ops and outage windows wait for the NOC.
  • The Databricks bill doesn't grow per tap. One scheduled job a day. No warehouse query per screen, no Model Serving call per visit.
Talking points. The last requirement is the one the operator's data platform team cares about. Databricks is excellent at producing the asset-health scores from alarms and telemetry. It is an expensive place to serve them from, 150,000 times a day, to phones that are half the time offline.
